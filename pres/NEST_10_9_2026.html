October 9, 2026
Thanks. I want to talk about a number that Russian state television repeats constantly: how many Ukrainian soldiers have died. What do Russians believe about it, and where do those beliefs come from? I will give the substantive results first, and finish with what the project suggests about how we can study Russia right now.

Factual beliefs shape how citizens judge their rulers

1

Is the economy growing? Is there progress in the war? Answers to such questions shape attitudes.

2

Autocrats control much of what citizens hear about these facts.

3

Research disagrees on whether that control works: exposure to state narratives shifts beliefs, but people may also discount and read between the lines.

What do citizens believe when the state controls the supply of factual claims?
Start with why factual beliefs matter at all. Whether the economy is growing, whether the war is being won, whether the opposition draws crowds: citizens' answers to these questions shape how they judge the regime. Under autocracy the state controls much of what people hear about them. The literature is split. Some work links propaganda exposure to beliefs and behavior; other work shows people discounting and reading between the lines. So I want a case where the state supplies most of the numbers, and a fact that people cannot easily check.

Consider beliefs about Ukraine's military casualties

Why it matters

  • State media often report on enemy losses to signal success in the war
  • However, such reports may be understood as strong resistance, or as evidence of Russian tactics
  • An ordinary news consumer can hardly establish casualty numbers reliably

Why not Russian casualties?

  • Safer to ask: Estimating the enemy's losses does not criticize the army or the Kremlin
  • Russian losses are costlier to discuss and closer to home: of Russians I asked know someone killed or missing
Independent estimates exist, so we can ask how far Russians depart from them.
State media use enemy losses to signal that Russia is winning. But the same number can be read differently: as strong Ukrainian resistance, or as evidence of Russian brutality. That is part of what makes it interesting. It is also hard for an ordinary news consumer to establish reliably, even though external estimates exist. I ask about Ukraine's losses rather than Russia's because they are less sensitive: criticizing the army is criminalized, and Russian losses are close to home. In my sample, 58 percent of the respondents who were asked know someone killed or missing, and that question went to about half the sample. Reliable independent counts exist for both sides, which lets me compare beliefs to something other than the state's version.

Three questions

1

How far do Russians go along with casualty numbers provided by the Kremlin?

2

Who departs from them, and who goes along?

3

Are these beliefs malleable? Can they be corrected?

First, how much people discount the state's figures. Second, what separates those who discount more from those who discount less. Third, how firmly the belief is held, which I test with an experiment. But first, what the supply looks like.

Here is the media supply, measured as items per day that state a casualty figure. A local LLM, Qwen 3.8 with 27 billion parameters, reads every Channel One evening segment and every Meduza article. It screens for military casualties, then extracts each figure and whose dead it counts. Channel One has about 0.66 segments a day with a figure for Ukrainian dead and 0.11 for Russian dead. Meduza has about 0.53 articles a day with a Ukrainian figure and 0.96 with a Russian one. So the contrast is that state TV talks about the enemy's dead and hardly about its own, while Meduza covers both. I leave out Meduza's daily live blog until late 2024: it is one very long digest a day, and nearly two thirds of them state a Ukrainian figure, which would make Meduza look far more active than its articles are. Counting days instead of items would also narrow the gap. The coding is still before the final validity screen.

Few Russians use independent media: 14 percent trust at least one independent outlet, against 67 percent who trust a state outlet and 70 percent who watch TV news. The survey measures use and trust, not what people read about casualties, so this is an inference. Independent outlets do report totals for Ukraine's losses, but often by quoting others: Western officials in 2022 and 2023, and later Russian officials. I have not yet quantified how many come from where, because the current coding does not record who made the claim.

The data are from an online survey fielded in March 2026. Everyone was asked how many Ukrainian servicemen they think have died in the conflict so far, and answered on a thirteen-point scale from fewer than 50 thousand to more than a million, with a hard-to-say option. Then they rated their confidence. Before that I measured Putin approval, whether people want the war continued or stopped, media use and trust, and ties to the war. Online samples in Russia are what they are, so I report what these respondents say rather than claiming to describe the public.

This is the control group, everyone who saw no figure. About 31 percent answered 300 thousand or less, the upper end of independent estimates. About a quarter chose the top of the scale, more than a million, which matches what state media said. Another quarter is in between with no clear peak, and 19 percent said hard to say. Among those who gave a number, the shares are 38, 32 and 30 percent, and 56 percent said at least half a million. Those above a million are also much more confident than everyone else, while confidence is flat below that.

Who departs from the state's numbers?

Control group, respondents who gave an estimate. Lines are 95% intervals; gaps in gray are not statistically significant.
Here is the share who estimate 500 thousand or more, by group. People who skip TV news and people who want the war stopped depart most from the state's numbers; war hawks and TV watchers go along with them. Trusting a state outlet goes the same way. Putin approval points in the same direction but the gap is smaller, 13 points with a p-value of 0.05, so I would not say it does not matter; the sample is too small to tell. Knowing someone killed or missing makes no detectable difference to what people think about Ukraine's dead, though that item went to about half the sample. Everything here is a correlation: people who want the war to continue may also be more willing to believe the enemy is losing heavily.

Seeing a specific number moves guesses toward it

While most subgroups were responsive to the low figure, Putin critics and independent news consumers resisted the high figure.
Respondents who gave an estimate. Effect: change in the share estimating 500 thousand or more relative to control.
Before the question, a random fifth of respondents saw no figure, and the rest saw a recent estimate: either 140 thousand, close to independent counts, or 700 thousand, inflated but below the state's latest totals. Each figure was shown with or without a source; naming a source made no detectable difference, so I pool them. [Press the arrow.] After the low figure, the share estimating half a million or more falls by 17 points. After the high figure it rises by 15. Respondents did not simply repeat the figure: 38 percent of those shown 700 thousand still gave a lower number, and roughly three quarters of those shown 140 thousand gave a higher one. [Press the arrow.] The low figure moved every subgroup I looked at in the same direction, TV viewers and hawks included. The high figure moved most groups too. Among critics of Putin the rise was about 3 points, and among people who trust an independent outlet about 7, against 18 for approvers. Neither small rise is distinguishable from zero, but these groups are small (a few hundred respondents each) and the difference between groups is itself not established, so I put it no more strongly than "did not clearly move". It is exploratory. Each person answered once, so this compares groups, not individuals changing their minds.

Information control shapes the order of magnitude for factual beliefs

1

Propaganda sets the scale. A majority put the toll far above independent estimates.

2

Discounting is real but partial. A minority repeats the state's numbers: most discount them while still overestimating the losses.

3

The belief is malleable. A single number moves answers substantially. The numbers that circulate regularly reinforce these beliefs.

4

Opinion is mainly divided by media exposure and war attitudes.

What do we learn about propaganda and public opinion? One: monopolizing the supply of numbers sets the scale people have in mind; 45 percent of all respondents put the toll at half a million or more, against independent estimates of roughly 100 to 300 thousand. Two: discounting is real but partial; 24 percent give the top figure, but only 31 percent end up at or below 300 thousand. Three: the belief is tentative. A single competing number moves answers by 15 to 17 points and the source label barely matters, so what counts is which numbers circulate in a person's information environment, filtered by how much people discount them. Four: for public opinion, these beliefs divide more along media diet and attitudes toward the war than along regime support, though the approval gap is borderline.

Three lessons for studying Russia in wartime

Regime support is only weakly connected to beliefs about the war

  • Both critics and supporters overestimate Ukraine's losses
  • Many supporters report realistic beliefs and are receptive to corrections
  • Approval separates beliefs less than TV news or war preference do

Group differences are informative even when overall belief levels are not

  • In autocracy at war, many beliefs may be unstable, loosely held, or misreported
  • Comparing groups shows who discounts propaganda or who is more supportive of policies, whatever the average

LLMs reliably extract nuanced numbers from media or speeches

  • Outlets use different vocabulary and conventions, which keyword searches handle poorly
  • Every figure can be linked to side, period, source, etc.
  • E.g., Meduza often quotes Russian officials on Ukraine's casualties → Kremlin's messaging reaches people through independent outlets
Three things. First, regime support is a weak guide to beliefs about the war. Even among critics of Putin, about half put Ukraine's deaths at half a million or more, against 62 percent of approvers. The approval gap is 13 points and borderline; the gaps for TV news and war preference are 29. So approval polls cannot stand in for what people believe about the war. Second, group differences are sturdier than levels. A survey at war has unknowns I cannot verify: who is reached, who declines, what people are willing to say. One sentence moved levels by 15 to 17 points, while groups differed by up to 29. That does not mean levels carry no information; it means ranking groups is the safer use. Third, LLMs are now good enough to extract nuanced numbers from media reports, each with its quote and whose dead it counts, so every figure can be checked. As one use: about 12 percent of the figures of 500 or more that Meduza states for Ukraine's dead also aired on Channel One within a day, against about 1 percent when I shift the dates by one to three months. Examples are Shoigu's whole-war totals, 400 thousand in December 2023, 444 thousand in February 2024 and almost half a million in April 2024, and daily claims such as 800 a day on 27 February 2024. This is a proxy: it matches numbers, not sources, it includes foreign figures that Channel One relayed, and it uses only Channel One's evening news, so it understates overlap with state sources. Attribution coding would make it exact.
